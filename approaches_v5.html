🧬 The Biological Approach argues that human behaviour has biological causes. To understand why people think, feel and behave as they do, we must look at genetics, brain structure, hormones, neurotransmitters and evolution.
🧬 Genetics🧠 Brain & neurochemistry⚗️ Hormones🔄 Evolution🔬 Raine et al. (1997)⚖️ Nature–Nurture debate

Approach at a glance

Core idea

Behaviour can be explained by biological factors: genes, the brain, the nervous system, hormones and evolutionary pressures. The body and the mind are one — mental states are brain states.

Methods used

Brain scans (PET, fMRI, MRI), twin studies, family studies, drug trials, genetic analysis, post-mortem studies. Highly scientific and empirical.

Applications

Drug therapies for mental illness (SSRIs for depression), genetic counselling, understanding criminal behaviour, neurosurgery.

Key debate

The ethics of neuroscience — neuroscience is ethical as it provides answers (understanding consciousness, treating criminal behaviour, enhancing neurological function, improving marketing) but unethical as it provides power over individuals and society.

⚠️ You need to be able to state and explain the assumptions. In a 4- or 6-mark question you must go beyond naming them — apply them with examples.

The Three Core Assumptions

1
Evolutionary influences on behaviour
Drawing on Darwin's theory of natural selection, the biological approach argues that many behaviours evolved because they increased survival and reproductive success. Behaviours that helped ancestors survive were naturally selected and are now part of our genetic inheritance. For example, the fight-or-flight stress response evolved to protect against predators; fear of spiders and heights may reflect evolved danger-avoidance. Universal behaviours found across all human cultures — such as attachment, cooperation and aggression in competition — are explained as products of evolutionary pressure. This is the nature side of the nature–nurture debate.
2
Localisation of brain function
The biological approach proposes that specific areas of the brain are responsible for specific behaviours, emotions and cognitive functions — known as localisation of function. For example, the prefrontal cortex is associated with planning, impulse control and decision-making; the amygdala with fear and aggression; Broca's area with speech production. Damage to specific brain regions produces predictable, localised deficits. Raine et al.'s (1997) PET scan study found reduced activity in the prefrontal cortex of murderers compared to controls — directly supporting localisation as an explanation for violent behaviour.
3
Neurotransmitters influence behaviour
Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that transmit signals between neurons across synapses. The biological approach argues that imbalances in neurotransmitter levels directly influence behaviour and mental states. For example, low serotonin is associated with depression; excess dopamine activity is linked to schizophrenia; low GABA is associated with anxiety. Drug therapies target these neurotransmitter systems — SSRIs increase serotonin availability in the treatment of depression — providing real-world support for the role of neurochemistry in behaviour. This also explains individual differences in emotional responses and vulnerability to mental illness.
🔬 Classic Study: Raine, Buchsbaum & LaCasse (1997) — "Brain abnormalities in murderers indicated by positron emission tomography"
AimTo use PET scanning to investigate whether the brains of murderers pleading not guilty by reason of insanity (NGRI) showed different levels of glucose metabolism compared to non-murderers.
Participants41 murderers (39 male, 2 female) pleading NGRI in California — the experimental group. 41 controls matched on age and sex — including 6 with schizophrenia to match the experimental group.
MethodQuasi-experiment using PET scans. Participants injected with FDG (radioactive glucose tracer) then completed a Continuous Performance Task (CPT) for 32 minutes. PET scan measured glucose metabolism (brain activity) in different brain regions.
Key findingsMurderers showed: reduced glucose metabolism in the prefrontal cortex (impulse control), parietal cortex, and corpus callosum (connects hemispheres). Abnormal amygdala activity (fear/aggression), reduced activity in hippocampus and thalamus. Controls did not show these differences.
ConclusionsThere is a biological basis to violent behaviour — brain abnormalities, not free choice, may underlie murderous acts. However, Raine stressed this does not mean violence is determined by biology alone — social factors interact with brain function.

Evaluation of Raine et al. (1997)

✅ Strengths

  • Uses objective, scientific PET scanning — reduces researcher bias
  • Carefully matched control group strengthens the comparisons
  • Important implications for the criminal justice system — challenges ideas of free will and responsibility

❌ Limitations

  • Sample only NGRIs — cannot generalise to all violent criminals
  • Cause and effect unclear — brain differences may be result of violence, not the cause
  • Quasi-experiment: no random allocation, cannot fully control all variables
  • Sample has disorder confounds (schizophrenia, head injuries)
⚖️ Contemporary Debate: The Ethics of Neuroscience — This debate must be discussed from ethical, social and economic perspectives. Neuroscience is ethical as it provides answers, but raises concerns as it provides power.
The four areas of neuroscience under debate
There are four key areas where neuroscience both provides answers and raises ethical concerns: (1) understanding consciousness — Koubeissi et al. (2014) found that stimulating the claustrum in an epileptic patient caused immediate, reversible loss of consciousness, raising questions about who has the right to alter or end conscious experience. (2) Treating criminal behaviour — Crockett et al. (2010) found that increasing serotonin via SSRIs reduced impulsivity and aggression, suggesting pharmacological treatment of offenders, but raising consent issues. (3) Enhancing neurological function — Kadosh et al. (2012) found tDCS improved mathematical performance in healthy students, raising fairness and safety concerns. (4) Improving marketing — neuromarketing uses EEG and brain scanning to reveal unconscious consumer responses; Goldman et al. (2008) found approximately 56% of neuroimaging studies produce incidental findings yet marketing firms are not obliged to inform participants.
⚖️ Ethical implications
Neuroscience raises profound ethical questions across all four areas. Using pharmacological treatment on offenders — as suggested by Crockett et al.'s serotonin research — raises issues of autonomy and informed consent: if medication is a condition of parole, the individual is effectively coerced. Martinez et al. (2004) argued that using brain evidence in criminal justice risks neuro-determinism — the idea that biology determines destiny — undermining moral responsibility and due process. Koubeissi et al.'s consciousness research gives clinicians the power to make life-and-death decisions, yet is based on a single case study that cannot be reliably generalised. In neuromarketing, consumers have not consented to having their unconscious preferences exploited commercially, and participants who undergo brain scanning may have undetected pathology that marketing firms — unlike medical practitioners — are under no obligation to disclose. Cognitive enhancement via tDCS may become coercive in competitive educational environments where students feel pressured to enhance to keep up.
🌍 Social implications
The social implications of neuroscience are wide-ranging. Brain scan evidence is increasingly used in courtrooms — Raine et al.'s (1997) research on murderers' prefrontal cortex activity has been cited in legal cases to argue for reduced culpability. This has significant social consequences: if brain abnormalities can explain criminal behaviour, the justice system must grapple with questions of responsibility, punishment and rehabilitation. The use of tDCS for cognitive enhancement could deepen social inequalities — if only the wealthy can access enhancement technologies, educational and occupational advantages become neurologically stratified. In healthcare, neuroimaging offers the potential to identify risk factors for mental illness and aggression early, enabling intervention — but also raises the spectre of pre-emptive action against individuals who have not yet committed any offence. Cultural diversity matters: different cultures hold different views about the relationship between brain, mind and moral responsibility, and neuroscientific findings must not be applied as universal truths without sensitivity to these differences.
💰 Economic implications
The economic dimensions of neuroscience ethics are significant. The UK estimated the cost of depression and anxiety at £23.9 billion in 2000 — neuroscience-based drug therapies and interventions that reduce this burden represent enormous economic value. If pharmacological treatment of offenders reduces reoffending, the economic savings in incarceration, policing and criminal justice costs could be substantial. However, cognitive enhancement technologies like tDCS, if commercially available, risk creating a two-tier society where wealthy individuals buy neurological advantages — with long-term economic consequences for equality of opportunity. The neuromarketing industry is worth billions globally — corporations invest heavily in understanding consumer neuroscience because it improves return on advertising spend. This economic incentive drives the use of brain scanning in commercial contexts without adequate ethical oversight. Conversely, the emerging field of neuroethics — establishing regulatory frameworks for how brain data is collected and used — requires public investment but could prevent costly legal challenges and protect individuals from exploitation.

Consider cultural diversity — different cultures hold different views about brain, mind and moral responsibility.

Evaluating the Biological Approach

✅ Strength: Scientific and objective methods
The biological approach uses highly scientific methods such as brain scanning (PET, fMRI), twin studies and drug trials. These produce quantitative, objective data that can be replicated and tested. This gives the approach high reliability and supports its claims with strong empirical evidence. For example, Raine et al. (1997) used PET scanning which provides measurable, objective brain activity data.
✅ Strength: Real-world applications
Biological research has led to effective drug therapies for mental illness (e.g. SSRIs for depression, antipsychotics for schizophrenia), contributing significantly to clinical psychology. It has also informed criminal justice policy — Raine's research is used in court cases to argue for reduced responsibility. These practical applications demonstrate the usefulness and value of the approach.
❌ Limitation: Reductionist
The biological approach reduces complex human behaviour to physiological mechanisms — genes, neurotransmitters and brain structures. This biological reductionism ignores cognitive, social, cultural and emotional factors. For example, depression is not simply low serotonin — it also involves negative thought patterns, social isolation and life events. Reducing it purely to biology overlooks the full picture.
❌ Limitation: Deterministic
By arguing that behaviour is caused by genes and biology over which people have no control, the approach implies biological determinism — that behaviour is fixed and inevitable. This raises ethical concerns: if violent criminals have abnormal brains, are they responsible for their actions? It also denies the role of free will and personal agency in determining behaviour.
⚠️ Limitation: Nature–nurture oversimplification
The biological approach tends to over-emphasise nature at the expense of nurture. The diathesis-stress model and gene-environment interaction research shows that biological predispositions only result in behaviour when combined with environmental triggers. An approach that only focuses on biology cannot fully explain why not all people with the same genes develop the same behaviours.

Description (AO1):
The biological approach argues that behaviour has biological causes. One core assumption is that genes influence behaviour — we inherit genetic predispositions from our parents that shape how we think, feel and act. Evidence from twin studies supports this: identical (MZ) twins show higher concordance rates for conditions like depression than non-identical (DZ) twins, suggesting a heritable component.

By explaining behaviour purely in terms of genes and brain chemistry, it ignores the role of cognitive processes, social learning and environmental factors. AO1 — Describe 10 marks
Describe the findings and conclusions of Raine, Buchsbaum and LaCasse's (1997) research 'Brain abnormalities in murderers indicated by positron emission tomography'. [10]
Structure: Both findings AND conclusions must be included to access the 6–10 mark range.

Findings to include: Reduced activity in prefrontal cortex; reduced left hemisphere activity in amygdala, thalamus, hippocampus and left angular gyrus; increased right hemisphere activity in amygdala, thalamus and hippocampus; abnormal activity in corpus callosum and cerebellum. No significant differences in many areas linked to mental illness — differences were specific to violence/aggression.

Conclusions to include: Link between brain dysfunction and predisposition to violence in NGRIs; violence best explained by disruption of multiple interacting brain mechanisms; Raine warned findings do NOT mean violence is biologically determined; findings do NOT mean murderers are not responsible; PET scans cannot diagnose people; brain differences could be an effect of violence, not the cause.
AO1 Findings

Raine et al. (1997) found that compared to matched controls, the 41 NGRI murderers showed reduced glucose metabolism (reduced brain activity) in several key areas. Most notably, there was reduced activity in the prefrontal cortex — an area associated with rationality, impulse control and self-restraint. Reduced activity was also found in the left hemisphere in the amygdala, thalamus, hippocampus and left angular gyrus (linked to morality). Conversely, there was increased activity in the right hemisphere amygdala, thalamus and hippocampus, and in the cerebellum. Importantly, there were no significant differences between NGRIs and controls in brain structures primarily associated with mental illness — the differences were specific to regions linked to aggression and violence.

AO1 Conclusions

Raine concluded that the findings support a link between brain dysfunction and a predisposition towards violence in NGRIs. Prefrontal deficits may increase impulsivity and emotional reactivity, while limbic system abnormalities may impair the ability to judge social situations accurately, potentially causing overreaction. However, Raine explicitly warned that the findings do not show that violent behaviour is determined by biology alone — social and psychological factors play important roles. The findings also do not mean that murderers are not responsible for their actions, nor that PET scans can diagnose murderers. Crucially, the brain differences found could be an effect of violent behaviour rather than a cause. Nevertheless, the study demonstrates a meaningful association between brain dysfunction and violent predisposition in this specific population.

AO2 — Apply 10 marks
Discuss why a psychologist from the biological approach would consider drug therapy OR psychosurgery as an appropriate treatment for mental illness. [10]
AO2 — Apply the approach's assumptions to justify the therapy.

For drug therapy: Link to neurotransmitter assumption (e.g. SSRIs raise serotonin → reduces depression); localisation (targeting specific brain systems); evolutionary basis of stress responses. Evaluate — success rates, relapse, treats symptoms not cause, side effects.

AO1 The biological approach assumes that behaviour and mental illness have biological causes, including imbalances in neurotransmitter levels and dysfunction in specific brain regions. Drug therapy follows directly from these assumptions: if low serotonin is associated with depression, then SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors), which increase serotonin availability at the synapse, should alleviate depressive symptoms. Similarly, if excess dopamine activity is linked to schizophrenia, antipsychotic drugs that block dopamine receptors should reduce psychotic symptoms. The approach would therefore view drug therapy as treating the biological root of the problem.

AO1 The assumption of localisation of brain function further supports drug therapy — by targeting specific neurotransmitter systems associated with specific brain regions and disorders, drug treatments are theoretically precise interventions based on known neurobiological mechanisms.

AO3 There is substantial evidence supporting the effectiveness of drug therapy. Meta-analyses consistently show SSRIs are significantly more effective than placebo for moderate to severe depression. Their widespread prescription by the NHS reflects this evidence base. However, critics note that drug therapy treats symptoms rather than underlying causes — when medication stops, symptoms often return (high relapse rates). Side effects can also be significant, affecting quality of life. Furthermore, not all patients respond to the same drugs, reflecting individual differences in neurochemistry that the biological approach must account for. Psychosurgery, while a more radical application of biological assumptions, has shown effectiveness for treatment-resistant OCD through deep brain stimulation, though the irreversible nature of some procedures raises serious ethical concerns about consent and risk.

🧾 CONCLUSION (memorise this):
Neuroscience is not inherently unethical, but the power it creates poses risks to autonomy, consent and justice. Therefore, it is only ethical when strong regulation and ethical safeguards are in place.

Neuroscience has transformed our understanding of human behaviour by identifying the biological mechanisms underlying cognition, emotion and behaviour. This knowledge provides valuable answers, but also raises ethical concerns because it grants significant power to influence, control and potentially exploit individuals. Whether neuroscience is ethical depends on how this power is used and regulated.

One way neuroscience is ethical is through its contribution to understanding consciousness and improving medical decision-making. Mohamad Koubeissi demonstrated that stimulation of the claustrum could temporarily disrupt consciousness, suggesting a neurological basis for conscious awareness. Core idea

Behaviour is influenced by internal mental processes including perception, attention, memory, language, and thinking. These processes can be studied scientifically, even though they are not directly observable.

Methods used

Laboratory experiments (controlled conditions), cognitive tests, memory tasks, reaction time studies, case studies of brain-damaged patients, computer modelling and neuroimaging.

🧠 The cognitive approach emerged in the 1950s and 60s as a reaction to behaviourism. It argues we need to study what goes on inside the mind to explain behaviour.

The Three Core Assumptions

1
Internal mental processes mediate between stimulus and response
Unlike behaviourists, cognitive psychologists argue that we cannot fully explain behaviour by looking only at stimulus-response links. Internal mental processes — such as perception, attention, memory and reasoning — influence how we interpret and respond to the world. These processes cannot be directly observed but can be studied indirectly through carefully controlled experiments. For example, Loftus & Palmer (1974) showed that the wording of a question (stimulus) affected memory (internal process), which in turn changed participants' responses.
2
The human mind works like a computer (information processing)
The cognitive approach uses the computer analogy — the mind is like a computer, processing information through a series of stages: input → processing → output. Humans take in information from the environment (input), process it through mental operations (processing), and produce a response (output). Memory, for instance, has been modelled like a computer's storage system (e.g. Atkinson & Shiffrin's multi-store model). This analogy made it easier to theorise and test mental processes scientifically.
3
Schemas influence perception and memory
A schema is a mental framework or package of knowledge built up from experience, which helps us to organise and interpret new information. Schemas can distort memory and perception — we tend to fill in gaps using existing knowledge. For example, a person who has a schema for a "kitchen" will tend to remember objects typically found in kitchens even if they were not present (Brewer & Treyens, 1981). This is relevant to eyewitness testimony — schemas can lead to reconstruction rather than accurate recall.

👁️ Contemporary Debate: Reliability of Eyewitness Testimony — This debate must be discussed from ethical, social and economic perspectives.
The psychology — why EWT is unreliable
The cognitive approach demonstrates that memory is reconstructive, not reproductive. We rebuild memories using schemas and post-event information, meaning they are inherently malleable. Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed that changing a single verb in a question ("smashed" vs "contacted") significantly altered speed estimates and caused participants to falsely recall broken glass. The misinformation effect means anything a witness hears after an event — from police questions, media reports or other witnesses — can contaminate their memory. The weapon focus effect further reduces accuracy: witnesses to armed crimes focus on the weapon, impairing their ability to describe the perpetrator. Wells and Olson (2003) found that jurors systematically overestimate the reliability of confident witnesses, even though confidence does not reliably predict accuracy.
⚖️ Ethical implications

It is ethically unjustifiable to convict someone on the basis of evidence that psychology has shown to be systematically distorted. The right to a fair trial is undermined when courts do not adequately account for the limitations of EWT. There are also ethical concerns about how witnesses are interviewed — leading questions used by police (as demonstrated by Loftus and Palmer) can inadvertently contaminate memory, potentially causing an innocent person to be convicted. Psychologists have an ethical duty to communicate their findings to the legal system so that these issues can be addressed. Each wrongful conviction represents a failure of justice: an innocent person loses their freedom, potentially for years or decades, while the real perpetrator may remain free. Families of both victims and the wrongly convicted suffer lasting harm. Loftus and Palmer's research has directly influenced police practice and legal reform — the Cognitive Interview is now used by UK police forces, improving the accuracy of witness accounts. Courts are increasingly aware of the limitations of EWT and some jurisdictions now require expert psychological testimony on memory reliability before EWT can be admitted.
💰 Economic implications
Wrongful convictions have significant economic costs. Re-investigating cases, conducting DNA testing and managing retrials all involve substantial public expenditure. When the real perpetrator remains free due to a wrongful conviction, they may go on to commit further crimes — creating additional social and economic costs. Conversely, investing in better interviewing techniques such as the Cognitive Interview represents a cost-effective prevention strategy: improving the quality of evidence at the investigative stage reduces the likelihood of wrongful convictions and costly appeals downstream. Training police officers in cognitive interview techniques and funding expert witnesses to testify about memory reliability in court are economically justified investments.

Evaluating the Cognitive Approach

✅ Strength: Scientific and rigorous methods
The cognitive approach uses controlled laboratory experiments which provide reliable, replicable data. By using carefully designed tasks (e.g. memory tests, reaction times), researchers can study mental processes indirectly with objectivity. This is a significant improvement over introspective methods used by earlier psychologists and gives the approach scientific credibility.
✅ Strength: Highly applicable — CBT and EWT reform
The cognitive approach has generated hugely influential applications. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) — based on the idea that irrational thought patterns cause mental illness — is the most widely used psychological therapy in the UK. Research on eyewitness testimony (e.g. Loftus & Palmer) has reformed police interview procedures through the development of the Cognitive Interview technique.
❌ Limitation: Machine analogy is limited
Comparing the mind to a computer is a useful but imperfect model. Computers do not have emotions, motivation, consciousness or social context — all of which profoundly influence human cognition. The analogy may lead researchers to overlook the emotional and social dimensions of thought. As AI research progresses, this debate becomes increasingly relevant.
❌ Limitation: Artificial laboratory tasks
Much cognitive research is conducted in highly controlled laboratory settings using artificial tasks that do not reflect everyday cognition. For example, watching a film clip of a car crash (Loftus & Palmer) lacks the emotional intensity of a real accident, reducing ecological validity. Findings from lab-based cognitive experiments may not generalise to real-world memory and decision-making.

The Three Core Assumptions

1

The behaviourist approach proposes that at birth, the mind is a blank slate — we are born with no innate knowledge, personality traits or behavioural predispositions. Everything we become is a product of our experiences and learning from the environment. This is a strong nurture position, directly opposing nativist views. Watson famously claimed he could take any healthy infant and, through training alone, shape them into any type of specialist — doctor, lawyer or even thief — regardless of their genetic background. This assumption means behaviour is highly malleable and changeable through altering the environment.
2

Classical conditioning (Pavlov, Watson) involves learning by association: a neutral stimulus paired repeatedly with an unconditioned stimulus eventually produces a conditioned response on its own. Watson & Rayner (1920) demonstrated this by conditioning "Little Albert" to fear a white rat. This means all behaviour — however complex — can be explained as learned stimulus-response associations.
3

The behaviourist approach assumes that the basic laws of learning apply equally to all species — humans and non-human animals learn through the same conditioning principles. This is why Pavlov's dogs, Skinner's rats and Watson's infant are all seen as valid models for understanding human behaviour. It reflects an evolutionary continuity assumption: there is no fundamental qualitative difference between animal and human learning. This has both scientific advantages (animal research is easier to control) and limitations (human cognition and language may make direct comparison too simplistic).

🔔 Contemporary Debate: Using Conditioning Techniques to Control the Behaviour of Children — This debate must be discussed from ethical, social and economic perspectives.
The case FOR — conditioning works and is widely used
Behaviourist conditioning techniques are widely used with children and there is strong evidence they are effective. Token economy systems — where children earn tokens for desired behaviour exchangeable for rewards — are used in schools for children with ADHD and conduct disorders and have been shown to significantly improve on-task behaviour. Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA) uses operant conditioning with autistic children and has RCT evidence for improving communication, social skills and adaptive behaviour. Positive reinforcement through praise, reward charts and stickers is deeply embedded in normalised parenting practice and recommended by child psychologists as more effective than punishment. Systematic desensitisation uses conditioning to treat childhood phobias at the child's own pace, with low dropout rates. Watson and Rayner (1920) demonstrated that emotional responses can be conditioned, providing the theoretical basis for these applications.
⚖️ Ethical implications
Using conditioning to control children raises significant ethical concerns. Children cannot give informed consent to behavioural programmes, making parental proxy consent the only safeguard — but parental interests do not always align with the child's wellbeing. Watson and Rayner's Little Albert study illustrates the problem: deliberate distress was caused to an infant who could not consent and the conditioned fear was never extinguished. Punishment-based conditioning is associated with increased aggression and damaged parent-child relationships (Gershoff, 2002) — it is now illegal in UK schools. The overjustification effect (Lepper et al., 1973) raises the ethical concern that rewarding children for intrinsically motivating activities undermines their long-term motivation. ABA therapy is contested by autistic advocacy groups who argue it suppresses natural autistic behaviour and prioritises neurotypical norms — raising questions about whose interests are being served. The BPS ethical guidelines require that participants are protected from harm and can withdraw — principles difficult to apply to children in behavioural programmes.
🌍 Social implications
The social implications of conditioning children are considerable. On the positive side, effective behavioural interventions can improve children's educational outcomes, social functioning and quality of life — particularly for children with ADHD, conduct disorders or autism. Token economies in schools have been shown to reduce exclusions and improve classroom behaviour, benefiting both the child and their peers. However, there are social concerns: the widespread use of extrinsic rewards in schools may produce generations of children who are motivated only by external incentives rather than genuine curiosity and intrinsic engagement. The neurodiversity movement argues that using conditioning to make autistic children conform to neurotypical norms is socially unjust — it prioritises social convenience over authentic identity. Physical punishment, once socially normalised, is now recognised as harmful — demonstrating that social attitudes to conditioning children can and do change over time as evidence accumulates.
💰 Economic implications
The economic case for using conditioning techniques with children is significant. Conduct disorders and ADHD are associated with poor educational outcomes, higher rates of exclusion and, in later life, increased risk of unemployment and involvement in the criminal justice system — all of which carry substantial economic costs for society. Effective early behavioural interventions that reduce these risks represent a cost-effective investment. ABA therapy, whilst intensive and expensive (costing thousands of pounds per child per year), may reduce the long-term costs of specialist educational provision and social care. Token economies and positive reinforcement strategies in schools are relatively low-cost to implement and can reduce the need for expensive one-to-one support. However, the economic argument must be balanced against ethical concerns — conditioning should not be used primarily because it is economically convenient, but because it genuinely serves the child's interests.

Evaluating the Behaviourist Approach

✅ Strength: Scientific and objective
Behaviourism focused entirely on observable, measurable behaviour, making it highly amenable to scientific investigation. Pavlov and Skinner conducted carefully controlled experiments with precise measurement — setting a rigorous standard for psychological research. The use of animal experiments allowed tight control of all variables, increasing internal validity.
✅ Strength: Practical applications in therapy
Behaviourist principles have been widely applied in clinical psychology. Systematic desensitisation (gradually pairing feared stimuli with relaxation) and flooding treat phobias effectively. Token economy systems use operant conditioning principles in psychiatric wards, prisons and schools. Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA) is used to support autistic individuals. These successful applications lend support to the core principles of the approach.
❌ Limitation: Ignores mental processes
By focusing only on observable behaviour and rejecting the study of mental processes, behaviourism presents an incomplete picture of human psychology. Cognitive psychologists have demonstrated that thoughts and beliefs (e.g. schemas, attributions) profoundly influence behaviour in ways that cannot be explained by conditioning alone. The emergence of the cognitive revolution in the 1960s was a direct response to this limitation.
❌ Limitation: Ethical concerns with animal research
Much behaviourist research — from Pavlov's dogs to Skinner's rats — involved animal subjects who could not give consent and were often subjected to stressful conditions. Watson & Rayner's Little Albert study involved deliberate distress to a human infant with no attempt to extinguish the conditioned fear. These raise serious ethical concerns about both the welfare of participants and the generalisability of animal findings to humans.
❌ Limitation: Environmental determinism oversimplifies
The strict behaviourist claim that all behaviour is environmentally determined ignores the role of biology (e.g. species-specific learning constraints), cognition (Tolman's latent learning) and free will. Seligman (1970) found that animals are biologically prepared to learn some associations more easily than others — contradicting the idea that all stimuli are equally conditionable. The approach presents an overly mechanistic view of human beings.

Bowlby (1944) extended this, arguing that the quality of early attachment relationships — particularly with the mother — shapes the child's internal working model and capacity for relationships throughout life.
2
The unconscious mind drives behaviour
Freud proposed that the mind is like an iceberg — the vast majority of mental activity occurs below the level of awareness in the unconscious mind. This contains repressed memories, unacceptable desires, and unresolved conflicts that we cannot access directly, yet which powerfully influence our feelings, thoughts and behaviour. The preconscious contains material that can be brought to awareness with effort; the conscious is what we are currently aware of. Evidence for the unconscious includes Freudian slips (parapraxes), dream content, and responses during free association. The goal of psychoanalysis is to bring unconscious material into conscious awareness so that it can be resolved.
3
The tripartite personality: id, ego and superego
Freud proposed that the personality has three interacting structures. The id (present at birth) is entirely unconscious and operates on the pleasure principle — seeking immediate gratification of instinctual drives (sex and aggression). The ego develops around age 2, operating on the reality principle — it mediates between the id's demands, external reality and the superego's moral standards, using defence mechanisms (repression, projection, displacement) to manage anxiety. The superego develops around age 5 through internalisation of parental and societal values — it is our moral conscience and ego ideal. Psychological health requires a strong, balanced ego; neurosis arises when the ego is overwhelmed by conflict between id impulses and superego demands.

👶 Contemporary Debate: The Mother as Primary Caregiver of an Infant — This debate must be discussed from ethical, social and economic perspectives.
The psychodynamic/Bowlby position — the mother is uniquely important
Bowlby's maternal deprivation hypothesis proposed that continuous care from the mother during the first two years of life is essential for healthy emotional development. He argued there is a sensitive period during which the infant must form a strong monotropic bond — one primary attachment with the mother — and that deprivation causes irreversible damage including affectionless psychopathy. His 1944 study of 44 juvenile thieves found that 86% of affectionless psychopaths had experienced early prolonged maternal separation, compared to only 5% of controls. Biological factors also support maternal primacy: oxytocin is released during childbirth and breastfeeding, facilitating bonding, and NHS guidance recommends breastfeeding — an activity unique to the biological mother.
The challenge — fathers, shared care and wider attachments
Rutter (1981) challenged Bowlby directly, arguing it is privation (never forming any attachment) rather than deprivation (separation from an existing attachment) that causes lasting damage. Schaffer and Emerson (1964) found that 29% of infants formed their primary attachment with someone other than the mother, and many formed multiple simultaneous attachments of equal strength — directly contradicting monotropy. Cross-cultural research from Israeli kibbutzim and African multiple-caregiver communities shows children can develop securely without a single maternal primary caregiver. Neuroimaging research has found that the brains of primary-caregiving fathers show similar patterns to maternal brains, suggesting caregiving competence is not biologically unique to mothers. Koluchova's (1972) Czech twins recovered fully from severe deprivation with good adoptive care — supporting the view that effects of deprivation are reversible.
⚖️ Ethical implications
The debate has significant ethical dimensions. Bowlby's hypothesis was used in the post-war period to argue that mothers should remain at home — effectively using psychological research to restrict women's choices. Feminist critics including Ann Oakley argued this was ideologically motivated, not scientifically justified. Ethically, using research to prescribe how families should be structured infringes on individual autonomy and reproductive rights. There are also ethical concerns about the guilt experienced by mothers who return to work when research implies this damages their child — even when the evidence does not clearly support this conclusion. Conversely, ignoring the genuine importance of secure early attachment — wherever it comes from — would also be ethically irresponsible, as the evidence that early relationships matter profoundly is robust. The ethical responsibility is to communicate findings accurately, without overstating them in ways that restrict freedom or increase parental guilt.
🌍 Social implications
The debate has directly shaped social policy on parenting, childcare and gender equality. Bowlby's research influenced post-war childcare policy — discouraging mothers from working and shaping hospital visiting rules for children. Today the debate continues around parental leave (should mothers and fathers receive equal leave?), nursery provision (at what age is group childcare appropriate?) and working mothers. The current social consensus, informed by Rutter and cross-cultural research, is that high-quality, responsive caregiving from any consistent caregiver supports healthy development. Policies that assume only the mother can care adequately are both socially unjust and scientifically unsupported. There are also implications for same-sex families, adoptive parents and single-parent households — all of whom can provide secure attachment without a biological mother as primary caregiver.
💰 Economic implications
The economic dimensions of this debate are substantial. If Bowlby's strong version of the hypothesis were correct — that only mothers can provide adequate early care — the economic implications would be enormous: mothers would be expected to remain out of the workforce for years, reducing female participation in the economy. The UK's gender pay gap is partly attributed to career breaks associated with childcare responsibilities. The cost of childcare in the UK is among the highest in the developed world, making return to work economically difficult for many families regardless of psychological guidance. Government investment in high-quality early years provision (e.g. Sure Start) is partly justified by research showing that good-quality non-maternal care produces positive developmental outcomes — a direct economic application of the evidence that the mother is not the only effective caregiver. Equally, the economic cost of poor early attachment — in terms of mental health problems, educational failure and involvement in the criminal justice system — justifies investment in parenting support programmes regardless of who provides care.

Evaluating the Psychodynamic Approach

✅ Strength: Pioneering recognition of the unconscious
Freud's contribution was revolutionary — he was the first to systematically theorise the unconscious mind and its influence on behaviour. Concepts such as repression, defence mechanisms and the significance of early experience are now widely accepted across psychology, even if Freud's specific mechanisms are contested. The broader idea that much of human motivation is unconscious is supported by modern cognitive neuroscience research.
✅ Strength: Influential applications
Psychodynamic therapy has been widely practised for over a century and remains influential in clinical psychology. More recent adaptations, such as brief psychodynamic psychotherapy and Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT), have stronger empirical support. The approach also gave rise to hugely influential ideas in developmental psychology about the importance of early attachment and childhood experience.
❌ Limitation: Unfalsifiable / unscientific
A major criticism is that psychodynamic concepts — the unconscious, id, ego, superego, repression — are extremely difficult or impossible to test empirically. Philosopher Karl Popper argued that Freudian theory is unfalsifiable: any behaviour can be explained post-hoc in terms of unconscious conflict. For example, if a person denies having an Oedipus complex, Freud would say this is itself a defence mechanism. This makes the theory impossible to disprove and therefore unscientific.
❌ Limitation: Methodology — case studies and retrospective data
Freud's theories were largely built on case studies of a small, unrepresentative sample of mostly middle-class Viennese women. Case studies lack generalisability, and retrospective accounts of childhood (often gathered through therapy years later) are notoriously unreliable. Freud's reliance on subjective interpretation rather than objective measurement makes his findings difficult to replicate or verify.

Description (AO1):
The psychodynamic approach, developed by Sigmund Freud, argues that behaviour is largely determined by unconscious forces. Freud proposed that the mind has three levels: the conscious (what we are aware of), the pre-conscious (accessible with effort), and the unconscious (repressed, inaccessible material that still drives behaviour). He used the iceberg analogy to illustrate that most mental activity lies below the surface of awareness.

Freud also proposed a tripartite personality: the id (present at birth, unconscious, seeks immediate gratification), the ego (rational, conscious, mediates between id and reality) and the superego (internalised moral conscience, formed through identification with parents). Psychological conflict between these structures produces anxiety, which the ego manages through defence mechanisms such as repression, projection and displacement.

Finally, Freud proposed that personality is shaped by experiences during five psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital). Unresolved conflict at any stage causes fixation, leading to characteristic personality traits in adulthood. The phallic stage (3–6) is most significant, involving the Oedipus/Electra complex, as illustrated by Freud's case study of Little Hans.

Evaluation (AO3):
A strength of the psychodynamic approach is its pioneering recognition of the unconscious mind. Before Freud, psychology focused on consciousness — his proposal that unconscious processes drive behaviour was revolutionary and has been influential across psychology, psychiatry and culture. Modern cognitive neuroscience has provided indirect support for the idea of unconscious information processing.

However, a major criticism is that psychodynamic concepts are largely unfalsifiable. Philosopher Karl Popper argued that a scientific theory must be testable and falsifiable — capable of being proven wrong. Freudian concepts such as the unconscious and repression cannot be directly observed or disproved; any denial of the theory can be interpreted as a defence mechanism. This makes it pseudo-science rather than true science.

A further limitation is its unrepresentative methodology. Freud's theories were based on case studies of a small number of middle-class, neurotic Viennese patients, making generalisation to the wider population problematic. The use of retrospective data and highly interpretive analysis introduces significant researcher bias.

Nevertheless, the approach has had significant clinical influence. Psychodynamic therapy remains in use, and newer evidence-based adaptations (e.g. Mentalization-Based Treatment) have demonstrated measurable effectiveness. The broader insight that early childhood relationships shape emotional development has been validated by decades of attachment research.

The question of whether the mother must be the primary caregiver is central to attachment theory. While early research suggested that maternal care is uniquely important, more recent evidence indicates that it is not the identity of the caregiver that is essential, but the quality and consistency of care. Therefore, the statement is largely supported.

One argument that the mother is essential comes from John Bowlby's maternal deprivation hypothesis. Bowlby argued that infants form a single primary attachment (monotropy), typically with the mother, during a critical period. His study of 44 juvenile thieves found a strong association between early maternal separation and later delinquency, with 86% of affectionless psychopaths having experienced early maternal separation, suggesting that disruption to this attachment leads to long-term emotional damage. Bowlby proposed that this early relationship forms an internal working model, shaping future relationships. This supports the idea that the mother is essential. However, this evidence is correlational and cannot establish causation. Factors such as poverty or family conflict may explain both separation and later outcomes, weakening the claim that maternal absence alone is responsible.

Furthermore, Bowlby's concept of a critical period has been challenged. Michael Rutter argued that it is privation (failure to form any attachment), rather than deprivation, that causes lasting harm. Evidence from the Czech twins showed that children who experienced severe early deprivation were able to recover following adoption into a nurturing environment. This suggests that attachment can be formed later, supporting the idea of a sensitive rather than critical period. Therefore, the mother is not uniquely essential, as other caregivers can fulfil this role if high-quality care is provided.

Additional evidence comes from studies of multiple attachments. Rudolf Schaffer and Peggy Emerson found that many infants form multiple attachments, and in some cases the primary attachment figure was not the mother — approximately 29% of infants formed their first specific attachment with someone other than the mother. This directly challenges monotropy and suggests that attachment is not restricted to a single caregiver. Cross-cultural research further supports this: in societies with communal caregiving, such as kibbutzim or extended family systems, children develop secure attachments without a sole maternal figure. This indicates that attachment depends on consistent caregiving rather than maternal identity.

Biological arguments for maternal primacy are also limited. While oxytocin release and breastfeeding may facilitate bonding, research shows that primary-caregiving fathers exhibit similar patterns of brain activation to mothers. This suggests that caregiving capacity is not biologically exclusive to women. Therefore, biology may support bonding but does not make the mother essential.

A key factor appears to be the quality of caregiving. Sensitivity, responsiveness and consistency are strongly associated with secure attachment, regardless of who provides the care. This supports the continuity hypothesis, as it is the stability of early relationships — not the caregiver's identity — that influences later development. This challenges Bowlby's original emphasis on the mother specifically. However, Bowlby's theory remains influential: his emphasis on early attachment as foundational to development is supported by evidence showing that disrupted early relationships can have long-term effects. The distinction is crucial — it is the presence of a stable attachment figure, rather than the mother specifically, that is necessary.

In conclusion, the statement is supported. The evidence does not justify the claim that the mother must be the primary caregiver. Instead, what is essential is that the infant forms a secure attachment with a consistent and responsive caregiver during early development. While Bowlby was correct in emphasising the importance of early attachment, his claim of maternal exclusivity is not supported by contemporary research.

The Three Core Assumptions

The positive approach is unique in psychology for its firm commitment to free will — the belief that humans are conscious, self-determining agents who make genuine choices about their lives. Unlike the behaviourist approach (which argues all behaviour is environmentally determined) or the psychodynamic approach (which argues behaviour is driven by unconscious forces), positive psychologists argue that we have the capacity to choose, reflect and change direction at any point. Seligman's positive psychology is built on the premise that people have genuine agency — they can choose to cultivate positive emotions, engagement and meaning. Acknowledging free will has practical implications — therapies like mindfulness and quality of life therapy work precisely because they empower individuals to actively shape their own wellbeing.
2
Authenticity of goodness and excellence
A central belief of the positive approach is that humans are inherently good and have an authentic drive towards growth, excellence and positive development. This is a fundamentally optimistic view of human nature — contrasting with the Freudian view of humans as driven by destructive instinctual impulses (id). Seligman argued that psychology should move beyond treating illness to actively cultivating human strengths and virtues. The positive approach holds that humans naturally strive towards growth and excellence when given the right conditions — a view supported by Myers and Diener's (1995) finding that personal traits such as optimism and self-esteem are the strongest predictors of wellbeing, rather than external circumstances.
3

The positive approach is concerned not just with treating mental illness but with understanding what constitutes a fulfilling, meaningful, 'good life'. Psychology should study human flourishing, peak experiences and optimal functioning — not just pathology and dysfunction. Myers and Diener (1995) demonstrated empirically that focusing on positive traits — self-esteem, optimism, personal control and social relationships — predicts wellbeing better than the absence of illness. Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi's work on flow — the state of complete absorption in a challenging, enjoyable activity — further demonstrates that positive peak experiences are a core component of the good life.

Key findingsHappiness was not strongly predicted by demographic variables such as age, sex, race or wealth (beyond a modest threshold). Happy people tended to share four core traits: high self-esteem, a sense of personal control (internal locus of control), optimism, and extraversion. Having close, supportive social relationships was one of the strongest consistent predictors of wellbeing. Engagement in meaningful activity (work, religion, community) also strongly predicted happiness. Adaptation was noted — people quickly return to a baseline level of happiness after positive and negative life events (hedonic adaptation).

The key predictors of happiness — self-esteem, control, optimism and relationships — are consistent with the positive approach's emphasis on free will and the 'good life'. Myers and Diener concluded that psychology should focus on cultivating these traits to promote wellbeing across populations, directly supporting the goals of the positive psychology movement.

🌱 Contemporary Debate: Relevance of Positive Psychology in Today's Society — This debate must be discussed from ethical, social and economic perspectives.
The case FOR — positive psychology is relevant and effective
Seligman launched the positive psychology movement arguing that psychology had focused almost exclusively on pathology and needed to study what makes life worth living. Myers and Diener (1995) provided foundational evidence: their large-scale meta-analysis of over 170,000 people across 16 countries found happiness is best predicted by personal traits — self-esteem, optimism, personal control and extraversion — not wealth, age or demographic factors. Csikszentmihalyi's concept of flow — complete absorption in a challenging, intrinsically rewarding activity — demonstrates that wellbeing involves positive peak experiences, not just the absence of suffering. These findings have directly informed the development of wellbeing interventions in schools, workplaces and healthcare settings, and the UK government now measures national wellbeing alongside GDP.
The case AGAINST — limitations and criticisms
Critics argue positive psychology has significant limitations. Myers and Diener's research relies on self-report measures vulnerable to social desirability bias, and their correlational findings cannot establish causation. Hedonic adaptation — the tendency to return to a baseline wellbeing level after positive and negative events — challenges the idea that happiness can be sustainably elevated through intervention. Cultural bias is a fundamental concern: the emphasis on individual flourishing, personal control and optimism reflects Western, individualistic values that may not translate to collectivist cultures where social harmony and group duty are central to wellbeing. Ehrenreich (2010) argued that positive psychology can victim-blame those who suffer from poverty or discrimination by implying their unhappiness reflects insufficient optimism.
⚖️ Ethical implications
The positive psychology movement raises ethical concerns about the potential for victim-blaming. If individuals are told their wellbeing is primarily determined by their own attitudes and mindset — as Myers and Diener's findings on traits suggest — those who suffer from poverty, discrimination or chronic illness may be made to feel responsible for their own unhappiness. This is ethically problematic because it ignores structural causes of suffering. There are also ethical questions about the commercialisation of positive psychology: wellbeing apps, coaching programmes and resilience training are sold to individuals and corporations, sometimes without robust evidence of effectiveness. Applying positive psychology interventions in schools raises questions about whether children can meaningfully consent to psychological interventions designed to change their emotional responses. Equally, there is an ethical argument FOR positive psychology — the BPS ethical principle of beneficence suggests psychology has a duty to promote wellbeing, not just treat disorder.
🌍 Social implications
Positive psychology has had wide social impact. School-based wellbeing programmes informed by positive psychology (e.g. the Penn Resiliency Programme) have been shown to reduce adolescent anxiety and depression and improve resilience — addressing a significant social problem given rising rates of youth mental health difficulties. In the workplace, positive psychology-informed wellbeing strategies have been linked to reduced burnout and improved employee engagement. The UK government's adoption of national wellbeing measurement alongside GDP — tracking life satisfaction, meaning and positive affect at population level — is a direct social policy consequence of positive psychology's influence. However, Ehrenreich (2010) argued that the "positive thinking" movement can have negative social consequences, creating a culture where expressing negative emotions is stigmatised and structural problems are attributed to individual attitude rather than systemic change.
💰 Economic implications
The economic case for positive psychology is compelling. The UK estimated the economic cost of depression and anxiety at £23.9 billion in 2000 — a figure that has grown substantially since. Positive psychology interventions that prevent the onset of mental illness or accelerate recovery represent a significant economic saving. Mindfulness — recommended by NICE for prevention of depressive relapse — is relatively low-cost compared to ongoing pharmacological treatment or long-term therapy, making it economically attractive for the NHS. Workplace wellbeing programmes can reduce absenteeism and presenteeism (being at work but performing poorly due to poor mental health), producing measurable productivity gains for employers. However, critics note a risk that the economic framing of wellbeing reduces it to a productivity tool: workers are encouraged to be resilient and positive not for their own flourishing but to remain economically productive — arguably serving employers' interests over employees' genuine wellbeing.

Evaluating the Positive Approach

✅ Strength: Person-centred therapy — highly effective
The positive approach's therapies have genuine empirical support. Mindfulness is recommended by NICE for prevention of depressive relapse, and research shows it measurably reduces anxiety and improves wellbeing. Quality of life therapy addresses satisfaction across key life domains aligned with the approach's assumptions. ✅ Strength: Positive, holistic and optimistic
Unlike behaviourism (which reduces humans to stimulus-response machines) and psychoanalysis (which focuses on dysfunction and unconscious drives), humanism offers an optimistic view of human nature that emphasises growth, potential and dignity. Its holistic approach — considering the whole person rather than isolated behaviours — is seen as more appropriate for understanding complex human experience.
❌ Limitation: Lacks scientific rigour
The positive approach is criticised for its lack of scientific rigour. Concepts such as "self-actualisation," "unconditional positive regard" and "peak experiences" are difficult to operationalise and measure. ❌ Limitation: Cultural bias
The positive approach's focus on individual flourishing, personal control and optimism reflects individualistic, Western values — particularly the idea that individual fulfilment and self-expression are the highest human goals. In collectivist cultures (e.g. East Asian, African), social harmony, duty to the group and family obligations may be prioritised over individual self-actualisation.
